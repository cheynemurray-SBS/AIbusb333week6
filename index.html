    AI in Information Systems

    AI isn't a separate system bolted onto the organization. It's speeding up every part of the information systems framework you've studied so far, from how data is processed to how leaders decide. This week you'll see where that's happening, how organizations climb toward it responsibly, and then you'll step into a CIO's shoes and build a roadmap.

    By the end of this module you'll be able to

    • Identify the five areas where AI is changing information systems and the governance question each one raises.
    • Place an organization on the four-level AI maturity model using evidence, and explain which decisions should stay with people.
    • Lay out a phased implementation plan that moves technical, organizational, and governance readiness forward together.
    • Build an ROI case for an AI investment in operations and defend it to a board.
    • Ask the right questions of any AI vendor before the feature reaches your users.

    Lesson 1

    The AI–information systems convergence

    Think back to the IS framework: data comes in, gets processed, supports decisions, reaches users through interfaces, and has to be kept running. AI now touches every one of those stages at once. Five areas carry most of the change.

    AreaWhat AI doesOn a university campusThe question it raises
    Data processingIngests, cleans, classifies, and prepares data at a scale that used to take whole teams.Student, course, and enrollment data sync between the SIS and Canvas every term.Is the source data trustworthy?
    Decision makingSurfaces patterns and speeds decisions, from credit scoring to clinical diagnosis.Early-alert models flag students who may not persist so advisors reach out sooner.Who acts on the flag, and is it fair?
    User interactionNatural language, chatbots, and voice replace menus and form-filling.A virtual help-desk agent answers password, Wi-Fi, and Canvas questions at 2 a.m.When does it hand off to a human?
    System maintenancePredictive maintenance, anomaly detection, and self-healing cut downtime.AV and network monitoring flag a failing projector before the 8 a.m. class.Who gets the alert, and who's on call?
    Business intelligenceBI shifts from what happened to what will happen, and what to do next.Enrollment and retention dashboards forecast next term's sections and housing.Does leadership trust it enough to act?

    Look at the last column. None of those questions is about which algorithm to use. They're about whether the data is good, who is responsible, where the machine hands off to a person, and whether people will trust the output. That pattern will repeat all week.

    The hard questions are about data, accountability, handoffs, and trust, not algorithms.

    Lesson 2

    AI-driven business operations

    Operations is where most organizations see AI's first real returns, because the work is repetitive, measurable, and expensive. Three categories cover most of it.

    Process automation & optimization

    • RPA handles data entry, invoices, and compliance reporting.
    • AI workflows route exceptions and approvals.
    • Process mining finds bottlenecks in real time instead of in a quarterly review.

    Intelligent resource management

    • Staffing is scheduled against predicted demand.
    • Cloud compute and storage are allocated automatically.
    • Smart-building energy systems cut costs 15–30%.

    Supply chain intelligence

    • Demand forecasting reduces inventory and stockouts at the same time.
    • Supplier risk monitoring flags disruptions early.
    • Route optimization (UPS ORION) saves millions of miles.

    A warning comes with the first category. If a purchase approval currently passes through seven signatures and three of them add nothing, automating it just produces bad approvals faster. Map the process, remove the waste, then automate what's left. Process mining helps here because it shows you how work actually flows, not how the policy manual says it flows.

    Automate the paperwork around a decision, and fix a process before you automate it.

    Lesson 3

    Implementation frameworks: the AI maturity model

    Organizations don't jump from spreadsheets to self-driving operations. They climb, one level at a time, and each level needs the one below it to be solid. Most organizations today are at Level 1 or 2.

    Level 1Reactive
    Follows rules humans wrote. Flags exceptions and runs scripted responses; nothing learns.
    Spam filters, fraud alerts, rule-based chatbots
    Higher ed: everywhere (phishing filters, "missing assignment" alerts)
    Level 2Predictive
    Learns from historical data to forecast what's coming, so the organization acts before problems occur.
    Demand forecasting, predictive maintenance, churn
    Higher ed: investing now (enrollment forecasting, retention early-alert)
    Level 3Adaptive
    Adjusts behavior in real time as conditions change, optimizing continuously against goals.
    Dynamic pricing, personalization, autonomous routing
    Higher ed: selected courses (adaptive courseware)
    Level 4Autonomous
    Makes and executes decisions within boundaries humans set; AI handles execution end to end.
    Self-driving vehicles, algorithmic trading, autonomous plants
    Higher ed: rare, by design (high-stakes student decisions without human review)

    Human in the loop

    The goal isn't Level 4 everywhere. It's the right level for each decision. On a campus, grades, admissions, financial aid, and conduct decisions stay with people. Those decisions must be explainable, appealable, and owned by someone accountable. Regulators agree: the EU AI Act (2024) classifies AI used for admissions and for evaluating students as high-risk.

    The same logic applies in any industry. A hospital can let AI reorder gauze automatically. It should not let AI discharge a patient on its own.

    Not Level 4 everywhere: the right level for each decision.

    Lesson 4

    Strategic implementation and governance

    A maturity model tells you where you are. A strategy tells you how to move. The pattern below works because it builds foundations before it scales.

    Foundation building

    • Data infrastructure and governance
    • AI literacy across the organization
    • High-value pilot use cases
    • Baseline metrics

    Scale & integrate

    • Pilots into production
    • AI inside existing IS workflows
    • Cross-functional data sharing
    • Internal AI ops capability

    Innovation & transformation

    • AI-native products and services
    • Autonomous process ownership
    • Competitive differentiation
    • Continuous experimentation

    Baseline metrics deserve attention. If you don't measure overtime, error rates, or cycle time before the pilot, you can't prove the pilot worked, and the board won't fund Phase 2.

    Three success factors that have to move together

    TechnicalClean, accessible data; scalable cloud or on-prem infrastructure; MLOps to manage the model lifecycle.
    OrganizationalAn executive sponsor; a cross-functional steering committee; upskilling and change management.
    Governance & ethicsBias audits; explainability matched to the use case; privacy compliance (GDPR, CCPA, and FERPA on campus; HIPAA in healthcare).

    A strong data platform with no sponsor stalls. A sponsor with no governance creates risk. Readiness has to advance on all three fronts.

    What governance looks like on a campus

    Approved toolsEnterprise licenses protect university data. Free consumer tools may train on whatever you type into them.
    Data classificationClear rules for which data goes into which tool. FERPA-protected records never go into unapproved tools.
    Vendor reviewSecurity, privacy, accessibility, and data-use terms reviewed before purchase and again at renewal.
    Teaching & integrityFaculty set course AI policies. Detectors can't stand alone, so assessment design matters more.
    AI literacyFaculty workshops and student spaces to learn and experiment with AI safely.
    AccountabilityEvery AI system has a named owner, and every affected person can question its output.
    Make the safe path the easy path.

    Lesson 5

    Operational efficiency: the best starting points

    Customer relationships, HR, and finance share two traits that make them good first targets: lots of historical data and clear metrics for success. You can tell quickly whether the model is helping.

    Customer relationships

    • Predictive lead scoring
    • Sentiment-based ticket routing
    • Next-best-action for reps
    • Churn prediction

    HR & talent

    • Resume screening at scale, audited regularly for bias
    • Flight-risk modeling
    • Skills-gap analysis
    • Personalized coaching

    Finance & accounting

    • Invoice matching and approval routing
    • Fraud anomaly detection in milliseconds
    • Cash-flow scenario forecasting
    • Automated audit trails

    The same functions on a campus

    Admissions & advancement

    • Engagement scoring: who to call first
    • Alumni giving-propensity models
    • Applicant chat around the clock
    Watch outA score decides who gets a call, never who gets admitted.

    Faculty, staff & student workers

    • AI-drafted job descriptions
    • Student shifts scheduled around classes
    • Accounts provisioned at hire
    Watch outAny hiring tool needs a bias audit and a human making the call.

    The business office

    • Invoice matching and routing
    • Unusual P-card charges flagged
    • Budgets forecast against enrollment
    Watch outEvery automated step needs a trail an auditor can follow.
    AI earns its place on repeatable work; people stay accountable for decisions about people.

    Lesson 6

    Long-term innovation: predictive and prescriptive analytics

    Analytics matures through four questions, and each one builds on the last. You can't recommend an action until you can forecast, and you can't forecast well until you understand why things happened.

    1. What happened? Descriptive analytics: dashboards and reports.
    2. Why did it happen? Diagnostic analytics: causal AI and explainability.
    3. What will happen? Predictive analytics: forecasting models.
    4. What should we do? Prescriptive analytics: AI-driven recommendations.

    This week's Dashboard assignment lives mostly at step 1. Notice how much more value is available as you climb.

    Case study: UPS ORION

    ORION (On-Road Integrated Optimization and Navigation) recommends a delivery sequence for each driver every day. It's prescriptive analytics at enormous scale.

    $300–400Mannual savings at full deployment
    100Mfewer miles driven per year
    100,000 MTCO₂ emissions avoided per year

    The math was only half the story. ORION's routes sometimes looked wrong to drivers who had run the same streets for years, and the savings depended on drivers trusting and following them. UPS had to invest in training, explanation, and change management to get there.

    The algorithm was prescriptive; the adoption was human.

    Future trends and generative AI on campus

    TrendWhat it meansHorizon

    LLMs embedded in ERP, CRM & SCMPlain-English queries replace SQL and dashboards; copilots are the first wave.Now to 3 years

    The shift from search to generate changes how people work with information systems. You stop hunting for the report and start asking for it.

    It's already here

    Generative AI is arriving on campus through four doors: copilots in productivity suites (email, documents, meetings), AI features in education software for course design and feedback, vendor renewals that switch AI on in software the university already licenses, and every student's phone from the first day of class. The question isn't whether to use AI. It's which AI, with what data, and who's accountable.

    Four questions to ask every AI vendor

    1. What data do you collect, and is it used to train your models?
    2. Where is it stored, and who can access it?
    3. Can we turn the feature off or limit it?
    4. How do we know when it's wrong, and then what?
    Not whether to use AI: which AI, with what data, and who's accountable.

    Key takeaways

    Case brief: Mesa Verde Health

    Mesa Verde Health is a fictional regional network: one 280-bed flagship hospital, one 90-bed community hospital, and three outpatient and urgent-care clinics. It has 3,200 employees. All figures below are case data for this assignment.

    Exhibit A: Systems today

    • The electronic health record (EHR) runs on premises on a version nine years old. The two hospitals run separate instances; the clinics use a third system.
    • There's no enterprise data warehouse. Each site has its own reporting analyst who builds reports in spreadsheets.
    • Nurse scheduling is done in spreadsheets at three of five sites. Supply ordering at two clinics is by email and fax.

    Exhibit B: AI already in use

    • Email spam and phishing filtering.
    • A rule-based sepsis alert in the EHR that fires on fixed vital-sign thresholds. Nurses report alert fatigue; many alerts are dismissed without review.
    • Pharmacy drug-interaction alerts (rule-based).
    • A vendor AI dictation tool piloted by 12 physicians. No formal vendor review was done.
    • Staff surveys suggest some employees use free consumer chatbots for work. There is no AI policy.

    Exhibit C: Operating pain points

    • Nurse overtime plus agency (traveler) staffing: $9.8M per year.
    • Medical supply spend: $41M per year; about 6% expires or is wasted.
    • Insurance claim denial rate: 11%. A 14-person team reworks denials by hand.
    • IT help desk: about 2,100 tickets a month, 40% of them password resets.

    Exhibit D: Constraints

    • The $4M covers software, implementation, training, and any new hires over three years.
    • The network has a privacy officer and HIPAA compliance office, but no data governance committee.
    • The Chief Medical Officer is supportive but wary after the sepsis-alert experience.
